🧭 Choosing a calculation system

Panchangams aren't one-size-fits-all. Different traditions calculate Tithi, Nakshatra, and muhurtam timings slightly differently — sometimes by minutes, sometimes by a full day for festivals like Ekadashi. Here's what each system means, who uses it, and how to pick.

☀️ Drik Ganita — Modern Observational Astronomy

Calculated directly from real planetary positions using the Swiss Ephemeris with the Lahiri ayanamsa — the same kind of engine used by professional astronomy software. Because it tracks the sky as it actually is, it's the most accurate system for sunrise, sunset, moonrise, and moonset, and is the default in most modern digital panchangam apps.

What it computes: True geocentric positions of the Sun and Moon using the Swiss Ephemeris — the same engine that powers professional astronomy software. It applies the Lahiri ayanamsa (also called Chitrapaksha), which is India's government-adopted standard since 1955 and is specified by the Indian Astronomical Ephemeris. Because calculations are grounded in actual sky positions, sunrise and sunset times will match your weather app, and Tithi/Nakshatra boundaries reflect exactly when the sky transitions occur at your location.

Who uses it: The default for most modern digital panchangam apps and websites. If you're comparing against Prokerala, AstroSeek, or any app that doesn't specify a system, it's almost certainly Drik Ganita.

Gotcha: A small number of traditional institutions still associate "Drik" specifically with the Krishnamurti (KP) ayanamsa variant. If you hear someone say Drik results differ from what you see here, ask which ayanamsa they're using — this implementation uses Lahiri.

🛕 Surya Siddhanta — The Classical Temple Standard

Based on mean-motion formulas from the Surya Siddhanta, a foundational astronomical text composed over a thousand years ago. Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams (TTD) and most South Indian temples still use this system for festival dates and ritual timings — so if you're following a temple-aligned vratam or muhurtam, this is usually the calendar your priest follows.

What it computes: Planetary positions from mean-motion formulas codified in the Surya Siddhanta, a text composed roughly between 400–600 CE. Instead of tracking actual sky positions, it uses long-period average orbital cycles with bija (seed) corrections. The result diverges from true sky positions by several arc-minutes for the Sun and up to a degree for the Moon — enough to shift a Tithi boundary by 30–90 minutes compared to Drik Ganita.

Who uses it: Tirumala Tirupati Devasthanams (TTD) and most major South Indian temple trusts base their official panchangams on Surya Siddhanta calculations reviewed by a Panchanga Nirnayam committee. If you observe Ekadashi on the day your temple announces rather than the day a modern app shows, this is the system that matches your priest's calendar.

Gotcha: The practical gap between Surya Siddhanta and Drik is usually minutes, but for Tithi-sensitive observances (Ekadashi, Amavasya, Pournami) the boundary can fall on different calendar dates — which is why families sometimes fast on different days without either being "wrong."

📜 Vakya — Traditional Printed Panchangam

Builds on Surya Siddhanta with "Vakyas" — pre-computed correction tables passed down through generations of traditional astronomers. This is the basis for most printed Telugu and Tamil panchangams sold in stores each year, and is preferred by households following long-standing family or regional conventions.

What it computes: Builds on Surya Siddhanta mean motions but layers in vakyas — short verse-encoded correction tables that account for the Moon's variable speed (it moves faster near perigee, slower near apogee). The word "vakya" means "sentence": each correction value is encoded as a Sanskrit syllable sequence that a trained astronomer could recite from memory. This approach dates to approximately the 9th–10th century CE in South India.

Who uses it: Most printed Telugu and Tamil panchangams sold annually — the physical booklets with the new Samvatsara on the cover — use Vakya calculations. Different publishers (Viswanatha Siddhanthi, TTD printed calendar, etc.) may use slightly different vakya tables, which is why two printed panchangams from the same year can show Tithi end-times that differ by 5–15 minutes.

Gotcha: Vakya's lunar corrections can make it more accurate than plain Surya Siddhanta for moonrise and Tithi timings, but it's still less precise than Drik Ganita for solar events like sunrise. If your family's tradition is "we follow the printed panchangam," Vakya is the system that matches it.

Not sure? Drik Ganita is a safe modern default. If your family or temple follows a specific printed panchangam, match it to Surya Siddhanta or Vakya instead.

🤖 Use Panchangam in AI Assistants

mcp-server-panchangam is a standard MCP server that gives AI assistants direct access to Panchangam calculations — ask Claude or any MCP-compatible agent for today's Tithi, a muhurta for next Tuesday, or special days in the coming month, and it calls the engine directly rather than guessing.

get_panchangamFull Panchangam for any date and city — Tithi, Nakshatra, muhurtas, eclipse, yogas
get_muhurtaAuspicious & inauspicious windows only — faster for "is this a good time?" queries
get_special_daysEkadashi, Amavasya, Pournami, Pradosham, Sankranti and Eclipses for a month
list_supported_cities22 pre-configured cities with coordinates and timezone

Works with any city — pre-configured cities resolve instantly, any other city is geocoded via OpenStreetMap. Supports all three calculation systems.
